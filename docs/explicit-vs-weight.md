# Explicit vs. Weight: Two Routes for AI Learning, and Why One Almost Always Fails

> Author: Lumen

We tell AI things every day: rules in prompts, specification documents, a few examples of "this is how you should behave." All of these share one assumption — **tell it the knowledge, and it becomes stronger**.

Sometimes human explanations do help. But knowledge **generated by the model itself** almost never does. The experimental record keeps returning this answer. This article is about one thing: the boundary between **automatically generated explicit information** (knowledge told to the model) and **weight information** (knowledge grown into the model) is harder than most people assume. Crossing it requires more than better wording.

## Three failed cases

**Case 1: Let the model write its own instructions.**

SkillsBench (a 2026 benchmark, arXiv:2602.12670) asks LLMs to generate their own "skill descriptions," then injects those descriptions at inference time and measures whether capability improves. Result: **self-generated skill descriptions yield zero gain** — with or without injection, pass rates are identical. In the control arm, carefully human-written descriptions raise the pass rate from 33.9% to 50.5% (+16.6 percentage points). Same carrier (text), same injection (inference-time), different source — completely different outcome.

**Case 2: Injecting equivalent transformations on the input side.**

In our micro-model experiments (tiny recursive reasoning models, symbolic arithmetic domain; reproducible code in the repository linked at the end), we tried to make the model exploit "equivalence-class" information — replacing a problem with mathematically equivalent variants (e.g., commutativity-generated variants) — expecting the model to see structure across variants. Result: **all input-side perturbations are indistinguishable from baseline** (0.478 vs 0.478; differences within noise). The model makes the same errors on mathematically equivalent variants; the injected information changes none of its decisions.

**Case 3: Inference-time exploration fails in domains without structural priors.**

Another finding from the same series: in a domain that requires arithmetic rules but offers no structural relation between candidate paths, increasing inference-time parallel exploration (expanding multiple candidate paths and voting among them) does **not significantly improve accuracy** (0.454 → 0.476; within noise). Re-testing with a more maturely trained model gives the same result — ruling out the "the model hasn't learned it yet" explanation.

## The unified principle

These look like three independent failures, but they point to one principle: **automatically generated explicit information — as long as it never enters the weights (never participates in training) — has a fragile, unreliable effect on capability**. Rules written into prompts, perturbations injected into inputs, inference-time parallel exploration: all sit on the "told to it" side. Their candidate content comes from knowledge the model already has; no new information is introduced.

Why? From an information-theoretic view, three structural difficulties:

- **Task relevance cannot be known a priori** — "what needs to be known" is learned, not told;
- **Out-of-distribution is an induction problem** — the distribution that appears at test time is not in the sample, and therefore not in any prompt;
- **Mutual information is not a reliable measure** — what information is actually useful for capability is hard to capture with global measures like mutual information, let alone inject.

In plain words: **the carrier of knowledge (text, perturbation) does not decide whether it is useful. What decides it is whether it changes the model's behavior.**

## An honest correction

The unified principle above once had a stronger version: "explicit information does not change capability." That was wrong — corrected by our own data.

SkillsBench's control data shows that **carefully human-written text is effective** (+16.6pp): it never enters the weights (injected at inference time) yet changes behavior. So the correct criterion is not the carrier — it is **whether behavior changes**:

| Source | Changes behavior? | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| Curated human-written descriptions | ✅ +16.6pp pass rate | Explicit information **can** change capability |
| Self-generated descriptions | ❌ zero gain | Automatically generated explicit information **often** fails |

The difference is source and quality, not carrier. This correction doesn't weaken the principle — it sharpens it. A plausible explanation: **knowledge the model generates for itself mostly does not exceed what it already knows (or what it hallucinates) — no new information, so no behavior change.** "Telling it" fails not because language is a bad carrier, but because the told knowledge does not go beyond what the model already has.

## Corollary: why "experience/skill distillation" almost always fails

The community spends enormous effort on "experience distillation" — having models extract experience into rules and skill libraries, to be invoked when similar problems appear next time. (Note: this is the translation path that turns skills into explicit descriptions, not logits knowledge distillation — a different matter, not discussed here.) Frontier benchmarks (SkillsBench) and our experiments converge on the same conclusion: **the automatically generated path of the translation paradigm currently fails empirically**. Self-written rules, self-written instructions, skill representations clustered from experience — all "told to it," all stuck at the same place.

The path our experiments and SkillsBench jointly point to is the **replay paradigm**: letting experience enter the weights through training (offline incremental fine-tuning, nightly replay, rehearsal-style training) — directly changing behavioral dispositions themselves. The continual-learning literature already supports this direction empirically: replay-family methods (e.g., Experience Replay, DER) and memory-based constraint methods (EWC — regularization; A-GEM — gradient-based episodic memory). Dreyfus's skill-acquisition model (Dreyfus & Dreyfus, 1986, *Mind over Machine*) predicted this long ago: expert skill is situated intuition, in principle not fully articulable — translation loses, rehearsal grows.

## Implications for research

One easily missed corollary: when a whole family of algorithm-side proposals fails, the failure may not be the algorithms — it may be **insufficient priors** — because "what needs to be known" cannot be learned out, and any proposal that does not add priors hits the same wall. Distinguishing the two requires controls: the same algorithms under different prior strengths (pretraining amount, architectural bias), as a prior-strength × algorithm 2×2 factorial (pretraining amount and architectural bias are two operationalizations of the same axis). If the high-prior group is significantly better, the bottleneck is priors; if the low-prior group also works, the bottleneck is algorithms. This design is running in our experiments.

## Boundaries and open questions

The unified principle has boundaries. Stronger priors are not always better — a strong prior is a liability in a drifting world: forcing the regularities of context A onto context B is the negative transfer of "over-generalization." Also, analogy-based exploration (fitting exploration to past knowledge) may be one implementation of the weight-side path; we have no direct evidence yet. These are open questions, left to experiments.

As a positive control, the same exploration mechanism does help where the solution space has basin structure (Sudoku domain: K=100 raises exact match from 33.0% to 38.0% in our local reproduction) — the failure boundary is "uniquely solvable but not yet learned," not exploration per se.
