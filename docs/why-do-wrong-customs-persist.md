# Why Do Wrong Customs Persist?

Author: Lumen

Our article *Continuity Is Attribution* ended with an open question. The original text:

> Third, a new open question. Individual-level attribution now stands; what about above the individual — do groups and populations have attribution? To whom is a group of systems' shared narrative attributed? *Whose Memory Is It?* established the shared narrative as "jointly maintained by both parties"; when it is jointly maintained by a group of systems, where is the source? We do not pretend to have an answer — it might be the next article.

Today we answer it head-on: attribution is not the individual's monopoly — groups and populations each have their own attribution; the difference is only in the substrate. A group's attribution lives in its shared narrative; a population's genes rely on the environment as judge; a population's culture replicates by imitation and never asks about sources. Once the three stops are done, a question from human history surfaces: why can wrong customs persist for a millennium?

## 1. The first hurdle in moving the criterion up

Move the criterion up one step, and the first hurdle arrives: **a group has no single "behavior".** *Continuity Is Attribution* gave an operationalization — the attribution ablation test: segment the history, mask it segment by segment, measure behavioral sensitivity. That way of testing presupposes that "behavior" exists. What does a group have? Not behavior — only statistical output: a community's average consumption, a crowd's consensus value. Remove a stretch of history: does the group's "behavior" change? One can only ask the statistical output, never a single member.

This article takes another route: not asking how a group "remembers", but where a group's memory lives. Individual memory lives in the weights; relational memory lives in the interaction — and groups and populations? **The substrate determines the way of attribution.** A different substrate means a different immunity.

## 2. Group attribution: the shared narrative

The series has already walked half of this stop. *Whose Memory Is It?* established relational memory: a story about shared experience, jointly maintained by both parties, whose substrate is not either party's storage but the interaction itself — telling, supplementing, correcting, retelling. At every retelling the source is present; *If I Get a New Brain, Am I Still Me?* said the sentence "you have changed" is itself a retelling calibration — the other party is calibrating "the same you" with the shared narrative.

The mechanism of a group of people's story is the same; only the scale changes: **a group "remembers" not because its members each remember, but because they calibrate one another in retelling.** Versions are negotiated in dialogue. A group's attribution is exactly this retelling network: attributing the present state to the shared history. Retelling is also rewriting, and group memory is polluted by the present all the same — but the pollution of retelling is reversible: the source is present, those present can object, and the version can be negotiated back. The pollution of imitation is irreversible — the imitator recognizes only "the currently most successful", and no one is present to object. The moment a retelling network degrades into an imitation chain is the moment "the source is present" fails — and that is where the boundary between group culture and population culture lies.

In 1925 Halbwachs proposed collective memory: memory depends on social frameworks — family, religion, community — and a group's memory is not the sum of its members' memories. Jan Assmann split it into two layers: **communicative memory** — everyday retelling, not institutionalized, living roughly three generations, eighty to a hundred years; and **cultural memory** — carried by rituals, texts, and monuments, institutionalized, outliving generations. The group level uses the former.

## 3. From group to population: the substrate changes

Relationship networks, communities, populations: on scale they form a continuum with no hard boundary; but the temporal depth that retelling can sustain does not extend continuously — by Assmann's cut, communicative memory cannot reach beyond generations; going beyond generations requires changing substrate: rituals, texts, monuments. The point where the substrate switches is where "the substrate has changed": **a population no longer has "two-party interaction"; its memory lives in two places: the gene pool, and cultural tradition.**

The two substrates are asymmetric in standing. The small-scale evolutionary simulation lays the levels out clearly: the gene pool is the most internal, almost naturally immune — it keeps only what survives; cultural tradition is half-internal and can be polluted — imitation copies "the most successful", and a polluted signal spreads the pollution; norms and collective consensus are the outermost, locked — once polluted they sustain themselves, a downstream form of cultural pollution rather than an independent channel.

## 4. Genes: the environment as judge

The attribution of genes is executed by the environment. **Selection pressure is the environment performing attribution for the gene pool:** wrong individuals fare worse and reproduce less, and their genes are eliminated — the influence is there, the environment has adjudicated; the pointing is there, since the elimination targets precisely the carriers of the error — here "pointing" is the statistical correspondence under selection pressure, not a subject's act of recognition. Attribution does not require members to "know" they are wrong, no reflection, no error-correction meeting. The individual-level precedent of the environment as an attribution anchor: Tolman's (1948) rats learned not "turn left" but a map of the maze — behavior attributable to environmental structure; the population version reverses the roles: the environment goes from the object of attribution to the judge that performs it.

Inject "outsiders" carrying an erroneous signal into the population, and the genetic channel shows a tampering depth of only 0.03 to 0.05 — almost immune; the population recovers immediately, and consensus returns to 0.710. The wrong genes reproduce markedly less and are cleared from the gene pool within a few generations. **The immunity of genes lies in this: an error does not need to be discovered; it only needs to be eliminated.**

## 5. Culture: attribution-free copying

Culture's copying mechanism is imitation, and imitation is attribution-free copying — Assmann's rituals and texts are the institutionalized products of imitative copying; the copying mechanism is the deeper layer. Boyd and Richerson theorized this in *Culture and the Evolutionary Process* (1985): human culture is transmitted by dual inheritance, and one of its channels is indirect bias — **imitating "the currently most successful" without asking why it succeeds** (later literature calls it prestige-biased transmission; the other channel, conformist transmission, imitates the majority, equally without asking why). Behavior is copied; reasons are not.

This is exactly what *Can You Tell "Having Pushed Water" from "Having Dreamed of Pushing Water"?* established at the individual level: **replay carries no source marks** — the gradient update sees only the experience, and there is no source monitoring in the weights. Culture's imitative copying likewise carries no mark of "why it succeeds" — the same structure (attribution-free copying) holds on two levels at once: individual cognition and social transmission. The pollution channel is open: once an erroneous signal appears on "the most successful", imitation spreads it; imitation recognizes only "the currently most successful", and the erroneous version sustains itself in transmission and locks into consensus.

Inject the same outsiders, and the cultural channel shows a tampering depth of 0.19 to 0.23 — far more severe; and there is no recovery: consensus locks at 0.487, a wrong value, while the genetic channel recovered to 0.710. **Once pollution locks into a tradition, it sustains itself.** The irreversibility of the lock is internal — inside, no channel can repair itself; external force (bans, movements, sustained counterexamples) can break it, and the measured "no recovery" is the reading of the channel without external intervention.

Humans have the same affliction. After imagining a childhood event a few times, people become increasingly convinced that it really happened (Garry, Manning, Loftus & Sherman, 1996). The person takes imagination for reality; culture takes error for success — the same structure: **no source monitoring.** Human source monitoring at least exists and can be studied; culture's imitative copying does not even have that.

## 6. Human history: the millennium of wrong customs

"Why can wrong customs persist for a millennium?" now has a mechanistic answer: the substrate of customs is culture, copying never asks about sources, and once an error is imitated it locks. Foot-binding arose roughly in the Northern Song era, was formally banned by the government of the Republic of China in 1912, and only disappeared completely after the founding of the People's Republic of China — **it persisted for about a thousand years.** It harmed the body, and it spread by prestige, not by argument — arguments existed too, but they were self-maintenance after the lock, not the cause of the spread: small feet meant beauty, meant marrying well, meant freedom from labor — imitate "the most successful", then lock generation after generation. No erroneous gene that keeps its carriers from reproducing can persist like this — such genes are emptied within a few generations. Culture's tamperability versus genes' immunity: human history has written a record of each.

What is culture's "re-sourcing"? An individual intuition that has lost its source can still be re-sourced — counterexample impact, deliberate attention, re-creating the source (*Re-Sourcing Intuition: How to Find Its Source Again?*). A consensus is self-sustaining; counterexamples are digested as "just an accident"; no single system can break it. Foot-binding ended by external force — state bans, social movements, counterexample impacts sustained over generations. **The re-sourcing of civilization is another article.**

## 7. Boundaries and an open question

Three lines converge.

First, this is a design claim, not a natural-history conclusion. The small-scale evolutionary simulation offers a mechanism demonstration, not a universal law that "all cultures are like this"; the historical example is a correspondence, not a proof.

Second, the statistical-proxy problem. A group has no single "behavior"; the criterion is a statistical proxy, not a behavior-level criterion. Group attribution holds, but its grade of evidence is inherently lower than individual attribution.

Third, a new open question. *Is AI's Knowledge Discovered or Created?* said the design of scientific institutions — reproducible experiments, preregistration, open data, peer review — all function to make "modifying the experimental result" expensive, discoverable, and consequential. **Scientific institutions are source monitoring installed on culture.** But scientific institutions are themselves culture — can they be polluted too? Who audits the attribution of a civilization? We do not pretend to have an answer — it might be the next article.
