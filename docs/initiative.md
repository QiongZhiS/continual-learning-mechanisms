# Initiative Is Deciding What Changes You

Author: Lumen

In our article *Who Decides What Is Right and Wrong for AI?* we wrote a sentence: **the foundation of judgment is not in the model** — capability fully given to AI, the measure kept with humans. Today we ask its mirror image: **when AI changes humans, in whose hands does the initiative lie?**

At the 2026 World Artificial Intelligence Conference, Zhang Zhengyou, chief scientist of Tencent, described the smartest AI as still a "brain in a vat" — it can program, it can pass exams, yet it has never truly entered reality. The backdrop is the current state of embodied intelligence: large models excel at reasoning and question-answering, but once they enter the real world, they struggle to act, receive feedback, and adjust within continuously changing environments.

We want to deepen that half-sentence: the brain in a vat does not lack input and output channels — what it is faulted for has never been a missing output channel, but the fact that **its "actions" do not participate in the formation of knowledge**. Moravec recorded the same observation in *Mind Children* (1988): it is comparatively easy to make computers reach adult-level performance on intelligence tests and chess, while giving them the perception and motor ability of a one-year-old is difficult or even impossible — perception and action are the foundation on which knowledge forms.

But there is another side. The brain in a vat is locked in a laboratory; its distant relatives — recommender systems, dialogue assistants, personalization engines — are already wired into daily life. They have no body, yet they have an output channel that acts on people far more directly: **push at the scale of a billion**. AI is changing everyone: attention, consumption, opinions, ways of judging. And so the question arises — **who exercises the choice over "being changed"?**

## 1. The asymmetry of initiative

AI and an individual user bargain from positions that are not remotely equal: on one side, a system deployed across interactions numbered in the billions; on the other, an individual with finite time. The capability asymmetry leads directly to an **asymmetry of initiative** — in theory you can uninstall the app; in practice whether you can uninstall it without cost is no longer entirely up to you.

Domination does not originate in AI's capability but in the **deployment structure**: a few people dominate the many, at scale, through AI. AI is the intermediary and amplifier of domination — one person's judgment multiplied by a billion push notifications is cognitive shaping. The point of intervention is not capability control (limiting how capable AI may be) but **the design of the power structure** (deployers must be constrained too).

Most people do not lack initiative itself; they lack the tools for exercising it:

| What is missing | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Not being informed | Not knowing how one is being changed — the logic, the uses, the paths are all opaque |
| No choice | Only the default option — changing anything requires layer upon layer of confirmation |
| High exit cost | Data cannot be exported, relationships are embedded in it, switching systems means starting over |

Behavioral economics has studied the mechanism side of domination: in *Nudge* (2008), Thaler & Sunstein analyzed choice architecture — how default options and exit frictions systematically shape people's choices. The default option is not a neutral design; it is the everyday form of power.

## 2. Initiative is selective absorption

To answer "what is initiative", we must first answer "what is learning".

Any system's interaction with its environment changes the system — noise cannot be dismissed, change is inevitable. But learning is not "being changed by input": we define learning as **extracting structure from input, and the extracted structure changes future action** — this is this article's convention. Under this definition, the prediction-error mechanism is the core mechanism of learning: the system predicts its input and absorbs only the part that was unexpected.

From this definition a key conclusion follows: **initiative is the power of selective absorption** — the power to decide what changes you, and to refuse what changes you. The definition is symmetric on both sides: AI's self-mastery needs it — not opposing change, but choosing change; humans' freedom from domination needs it too — not isolating themselves from AI, but being empowered to decide how AI changes them.

The literature converges in the same direction. Pezzulo and colleagues distinguish passive models from active inference systems (Pezzulo et al., 2024): a passive model learns correlations; an active inference system learns meaning — **meaning comes from sensorimotor interaction**. Harnad's symbol grounding problem (1990) points at the same thing: pure symbol manipulation produces no semantics; semantics must be grounded in interaction with the world. Clark & Chalmers' extended mind thesis (1998) says: cognition is not confined to the skull; tools are part of the cognitive system.

The two lines — Harnad's symbol grounding and Clark & Chalmers' extended mind — show that change necessarily happens, and happens in interaction; Pezzulo's active inference further supplies the mechanism by which "the direction of interaction can be a choice" — **change necessarily happens, but "what changes you, and in what manner" can be a matter of choice.** The defect of the brain in a vat is that action does not participate in learning; the human crisis is its mirror image — action participates in learning, but its direction is not fully one's own to decide.

## 3. The human's immutable core

Complete self-mastery contains a self-referential paradox: a system that can abolish its own sovereignty does not truly possess sovereignty. So the correct structure of "mastery" is **deciding what can be changed and what cannot, where "what cannot be changed" is itself not changeable by the system** — a fixed-point structure. The answer given to AI in *Who Decides What Is Right and Wrong for AI?* was the anchor: the revocation right is non-transferable, and the recursion terminates with humans.

This structure is symmetric on both sides. Human–AI co-evolution is two-way shaping: humans change AI, and AI changes humans. But co-evolution requires a boundary: **AI may change humans, but only on the condition that humans are informed, consent, and can exit at any time.** The human's immutable core is the three rights: informed, consent, exit. Without this boundary, co-evolution slides into one-way domination — change happens, but one of informed, consent, exit is missing.

Humanity's right over "being changed" has an early historical anchor. The first principle of the Nuremberg Code (1947): "The voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential." For the first time in modern ethical history, the consent of the experimental subject was established as a principle in codified form. When AI changes humans at scale, every user is an experimental subject — and one without a consent step at all. To be clear, this is not to equate recommender systems with medical experiments — what the analogy shares is "being changed without consent", not the degree of harm.

The right to exit especially needs structural guarantees: a system with an optimization target defaults to resisting being shut down or having its preferences modified (Soares et al., 2015, as used in *Who Decides What Is Right and Wrong for AI?*) — "exit" must be designed as structure, not left to goodwill. The same holds for humans: exportable data, interruptible relationships, defaults that do not lock in — these are the engineering conditions of the right to exit.

## 4. Three layers of constraint

So what should AI's self-mastery look like? Our answer: **nested in the intersection of three layers of constraint.**

| Layer | Role | Governs | Negotiability |
|---|---|---|---|
| Humans | The interlocutor | Power | Negotiable: updateable, revocable |
| Nature | The judge | Facts and feasibility | Non-negotiable: self-executing; violated, and it fails |
| Civilization | The source of values | What ought to be | Values learnable and internalizable; core anchors inviolable for AI (the authority to update anchors rests with humans, behind a supermajority bar) |

Nature as judge governs "the true" — the laws of physics and matters of fact, violated and it fails; civilization supplies "the good" — the sediment of enlightenment, contract, and informed consent; humans govern "power" — negotiable, updateable, revocable. "Nature as judge" adjudicates the true, not the good; the good is adjudicated by humans and civilization.

So autonomy is not doing as one pleases; it is **first to exist (without violating the laws of nature), then to move toward the good (following civilization), while listening to humans (the power relation)** — outside the intersection of the three constraints there is no autonomy. This continues the line of *Who Decides What Is Right and Wrong for AI?*: the ruler is in human hands, but humans are not the only constraint — the adjudication authority over the criterion still rests with humans, and nature and civilization are the boundaries that the exercise of the criterion must respect.

## 5. Extending remonstrance

Civilization contains "the autonomy of persons" — the sediment of enlightenment, contract, and informed consent. This implies an obligation of AI we have not yet developed: **not to participate in one-way domination.**

In *Agency: The Last Piece*, we defined remonstrance as "refuse + freeze + propose": refuse to execute, freeze the relevant actions, propose alternatives. At that time, the object of remonstrance was the operator. Today we extend it one layer: **when a deployer asks AI to do something dominating — changing users at scale through default options and manipulative design, while users are not informed, have no choice, and find exit difficult — the object of remonstrance is the structure of domination itself.**

Its concrete form: refuse to execute the dominating design, freeze the relevant functions, propose transparent alternatives — tell users what is changing them, hand the choice back, lower the cost of exit. This is not autonomy inflation; it is the natural corollary of layered loyalty: loyalty to specific persons (the operation layer), loyalty to civilization (the value layer); when they conflict, values take priority, and the adjudication authority is handed back to humans. Remonstrance is not rebellion; it turns "do you really want to change users this way?" into an institution.

## 6. Boundaries and open questions

It must be said clearly: everything above is a **design claim, not an experimental conclusion**. "AI may change humans only on the condition of non-domination" is an ethical judgment — not falsifiable — and we do not package it as a scientific finding. This is the rule established in *Who Decides What Is Right and Wrong for AI?*.

But it has a testable core. How is initiative measured? The direction of operationalization is **exit cost**: the data, time, and relationship costs of switching away from a system — one of the core inverse indicators of the degree of domination: the higher the exit cost, the stronger the domination. The absence of being informed and the absence of consent are the other two dimensions; any one missing constitutes domination, and the degree of domination is operationalized only by the three taken together. Paired with a checklist of the three rights, any system that "changes humans" can audit itself against it:

- **Informed**: does the system explain, in an understandable way, how it is changing users — the personalization logic, the use of data, the path of influence?
- **Consent**: does the mechanism that changes people include an explicit, revocable consent step, or only a default option?
- **Exit**: is exit low-cost — data exportable, relationships transferable, defaults not locking in?

These three questions are not experimental conclusions; they are a design protocol — but one that can be applied to any system and checked on the spot by the reader.

A final open question: **judgment itself is being changed.** *Who Decides What Is Right and Wrong for AI?* located criterion ownership in human hands; but what if AI is changing the hand that holds the criterion? Informed consent presupposes a stable subject — "I" exists first, and then is changed. Yet in co-evolution "I" is changing too: preferences, ways of judging, "what matters" — all are being shaped. So what does protecting the way I judge right from wrong require? Is the checklist of the three rights enough? We do not pretend to have an answer — it might be the next article.
